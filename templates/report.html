§ 01

Introduction

1.1 Overview

Traditionally, financial advising required a personal advisor to determine a client’s risk tolerance, assess market conditions, and translate the two into a coherent investment strategy. This process can be expensive, surprisingly inconsistent, and even largely inaccessible to investors without significant assets. The introduction of ‘robo-advisors’ has begun to automate this workflow, but many current solutions remain a ‘black-box’. These robo-advisors rely on coarse risk categorizations, and rarely discuss the assumptions that are necessary in their portfolio construction logic.

This capstone project introduces a three-component data science pipeline designed to address these current limitations and move the domain forward in a meaningful way. The first component aims to estimate a client’s financial risk tolerance from survey-based behavioural and demographic features, producing a continuous risk score grounded in representative data. The second component forecasts near-term sector-level volatility, providing estimates of uncertainty in the market that extend beyond just historical averages. This uncertainty is then used to determine an expected return for each sector of the securities market. The third component combines the outputs to construct an individualized portfolio using constrained mean-variance optimization intending to maximize the risk-adjusted return (Sharpe ratio) within boundaries that reflect the individual's estimated risk profile.

The contribution of this work to the financial domain is not any single model, but the integration of these components into a smooth end-to-end system from an individual’s personal characteristics to a quantitatively derived personalized portfolio. Each component is independently evaluated, but the pipeline is designed to be assessed as a whole. The value of the system lies in how well each component hands off accurate data as much as the performance of each in isolation.

1.2.2 Volatility Forecasting

Prior to Engle (1982) and his introduction of the autoregressive conditional heteroscedastic (ARCH) class of stochastic processes, econometric models assumed a "constant one-period forecast variance." Engle's observation that the future variance of a stochastic process depends on recent past values paved the way for new approaches in volatility forecasting. Many recent studies have successfully employed Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) deep-learning models to estimate future volatility, exploiting such models' ability to learn dependencies and relationships between variables over time. Assaf, et al. (2022) found that an LSTM model was able to forecast overall market volatility with performance on par with modern econometric models based on the ARCH process. Zhang, et al. (2025) combined a hybrid deep-learning model (consisting of a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) layer, a bidirectional LSTM layer, and an attention mechanism) with an ARCH-based econometric model to forecast volatility of the Shanghai Composite Index (SSEC), claiming to outperform a variety of alternative models including decision tree based ensembles. Most recently, Peter, et al. (2026) integrated a generalized ARCH model with an LSTM model in a stacked ensemble, claiming to demonstrate "consistent superiority" across a variety of metrics.

Our study takes a novel approach by focusing volatility for specific market sectors in the U.S. economy, represented by a selection of exchange traded funds (ETFs), rather than overall market volatility. Furthermore, we establish a practical benchmark by comparing our model's performance in forecasting volatility over a 30-day future window to the performance of the Cboe Volatility Index (ticker: ^VIX) -- widely considered the "the most popular gauge" of future (30-day) market volatility (Schwab, 2025).

1.2.3 Portfolio Optimization

The foundation of modern portfolio construction is Harry Markowitz’s 1952 mean-variance framework. Markowitz demonstrated that a rational investor, given a set of assets with known expected returns and covariances, should select a portfolio that maximises their return for a given level of variance. Variance can be directly related to risk, so this can viewed equally as minimizing risk for a given expected return of the portfolio. In theory, he suggests that there is an "efficient frontier" of portfolios that minimize risk for all expected returns and any portfolio lying below this line is considered suboptimal because a better risk-return is achievable (Markowitz, 1952).

A key metric derived from this framework is the risk-adjusted return (Sharpe ratio), which normalizes excess return above the risk-free rate (i.e., return on a no-risk asset) by portfolio volatility (Sharpe, 1966). Maximizing the Sharpe ratio is a common objective in portfolio management because it selects the portfolio on the efficient frontier that offers the greatest return per unit of risk.

Unfortunately, the classical mean-variance framework carries well documented limitations. Estimated expected returns and covariances are sensitive to the historical window and sampling period used, and small variations in inputs can produce dramatically different optimal portfolios. Robust optimizations need to explicitly account for uncertainty in the parameter estimates so that they perform acceptably well across a range of scenarios as opposed to optimizing for a single point estimate.

Practical construction also requires incorporating real-world constraints that the unconstrained efficient frontier ignores. These include upper and lower bounds on individual assets (to prevent extreme concentration in a single asset), sector-level exposure limits, and minimum diversification requirements. These constraints are now standard in applied portfolio management but the choice of constraint is not just technical, it encodes the investment philosophy and risk tolerance of the client. This is precisely the link our pipeline is attempting to formalize, by deriving portfolio constraints directly from an estimated risk score rather than a general risk bucket.
