The Risky Business of Financial Matchmaking:

Optimizing Portfolio Return and Volatility Within Investor Risk Tolerance

Authors Peter King, Ryan Peet, Kristine Zhou, Thomas MacPherson Course SIADS 699 - Capstone Status Final
§ 01

Introduction

1.1 Overview

Financial advising is traditionally expensive, inconsistent, and largely inaccessible to investors without significant wealth. While robo-advisors have begun to automate the process, most continue to operate as black boxes. They rely on coarse risk categories and obscure assumptions behind their recommendations. This project introduces a three-component pipeline to address this gap. First, a risk tolerance classifier that produces a continuous client risk score from nationally representative survey data. Secondly, a volatility forecasting model whose predictions are used to derive forward-looking expected returns by a Sharpe-ratio-based estimation approach for each instrument. Finally a constrained mean-variance optimizer that combines these inputs into an individualized, risk-calibrated portfolio. The central contribution is not any single model but the end-to-end integration of all three, with results presented through an interactive client dashboard.

1.2 Background

1.2.1 Risk Tolerance Estimation

A risk tolerance score for each client was calculated using a combination of self-reported behavioral and financial factors, following the general approach used by Grable and Chatterjee (2026), which observed that stated-preference questions do reasonably well in describing a person’s willingness to take financial risks, along with several demographic and socioeconomic factors. Our project uses PCA and Lasso to reduce the dimensionality of the data needed to predict a client’s risk tolerance score.

1.2.2 Volatility Forecasting

Many recent studies have successfully employed Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) deep-learning models to estimate market volatility (Assaf, et al. (2022), Zhang, et al. (2025), and Peter, et al. (2026)). Our study takes a novel approach by forecasting volatility for specific sectors in the U.S. economy, represented by a selection of exchange traded funds (ETFs), rather than overall market volatility. Furthermore, we establish a practical benchmark by comparing our model's performance to the Cboe Volatility Index (ticker: ^VIX) -- widely considered the "the most popular gauge" of future (30-day) market volatility for the S&P 500 (Schwab, 2025).

1.2.3 Portfolio Optimization

Portfolio construction in this project is grounded in Markowitz’s (1952) mean variance framework, which states that a rational investor should try to maximize their return for a given level of risk. A common metric derived from this is the Sharpe ratio, which measures return per unit of risk above a risk-free baseline (Sharpe, 1966). In practice, classic mean-variance optimization is highly sensitive to input estimates, often producing unstable portfolios (Michaud, 1989), and requires boundaries on individual assets and specific diversification requirements to prevent extreme concentration in a single asset. In this pipeline, those constraints are driven directly from a modelled client risk score.

§ 02

Problem Definition, Approach, and Methodology

2.1 The Challenge

The challenge was to build an integrated data science pipeline that translates individualized investor profiles into a personalized investment strategy through risk scoring, volatility forecasting, and constrained portfolio optimization.

2.2 Sub-Problems

2.2.1 Estimating Client Risk Tolerance

For the first component of our portfolio generation pipeline, our goal was to predict an individual investor’s risk tolerance using household and demographic characteristics which were observed within the Survey for Consumer Finances (SCF). Understanding a client’s appetite for risk is important in the application of any portfolio recommendation system because it influences how a portfolio should be balanced between pursuing larger returns and limiting potential losses. Because the SCF contains many non-correlated variables which could affect how an individual perceives financial risk, but no direct measure of financial risk-taking, our goal with the risk tolerance estimation and model was to use surveyable household features to estimate how much investment risk an individual may be comfortable taking.

2.2.2 Forecasting Sector Volatility

For the second component of our portfolio generation pipeline, our goal was to forecast realized volatility (also referred to as historical volatility) for a collection of sector-representative ETFs over a 21 business day (30 calendar day) outlook. A brief discussion of return and volatility is included in the appendix to this report. Our goals were to achieve forecast accuracy for the S&P ETF (ticker: SPY) on par or superior to the VIX and to create dedicated forecasts for other market sectors that would provide a more accurate estimation of those sectors' volatility than could be obtained by simply referencing the VIX.

2.2.3 Maximizing Return while Limiting Portfolio Volatility

Given a client’s estimated risk score and a set of sector-level volatility forecasts, the third component of our pipeline constructs a portfolio designed to maximize the risk-adjusted return within constraints calibrated to the client by maximizing the Sharpe ratio of the resulting portfolio. Refer to the appendix for a detailed discussion of the portfolio optimization problem.

Tier 1 — Asset-Class Level
  • Age-adjusted equity ceiling (110 − age baseline, modified by risk score)
  • Gold cap inverse-scaled with risk score (15% at score 1 → 5% at score 10)
  • Volatility band: floor and ceiling both scale linearly with risk score
Tier 2 — Intra-Bucket Level
  • Any single equity sector ≤ 30% of total equity bucket
  • Any IG fixed-income instrument ≤ 50% of fixed-income allocation
  • HYG ≤ (risk score / 10)² × 35% of FI bucket (quadratic scaling)

2.3 Datasets

2.3.1 Survey of Consumer Finances (2022)

Data for client risk tolerance estimation came from the Survey for Consumer Finances (SCF), a survey conducted every three years by the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System.

2.3.2 Financial Data: Yahoo Finance & FRED

Our financial data came from two sources: Yahoo Finance (via the "yfinance" Python API) and the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) database (via the "fredapi" Python API). For a detailed description of these datasets, refer to the appendix.

2.4 Methodology

2.4.1 Risk Tolerance Estimation

Data cleaning, imputation, and transformation

Our final Python methodology encompassed the download of the SCF Stata data directly from the Federal Reserve website, which included the primary survey data, supplemental extract files, and replicate weight files used for survey analysis. Each dataset was then standardized to consistent lowercase column names and then validated to ensure row counts and identifiers were consistent across files.

One of the main preprocessing steps involved separating the SCF into each of its five implicates, where each implicate was extracted using the household identifier structure and then merged with the supplementary data using common keys. Once the data was verified, the five implicates were concatenated into a single modeling dataset to preserve the full range of imputed household responses in the survey.

Feature selection, Feature extraction

Because SCF contains thousands of variables, many of the fields were overly specific or not directly related to investor risk profiling. A more focused feature set was necessary to improve the interpretability and usability of our platform.

For the target variable, PCA was used as a feature extraction method to combine multiple behavioral variables into a latent measure of financial risk tolerance. This feature selection combined with PCA provided a sound proxy target for supervised learning in the absence of a specific measure related to financial risk tolerance.

Separate feature selection was then conducted for the Random Forest model used to predict the PCA-derived risk score. For this method, emphasis was placed on variables that were meaningful from an economic perspective, explainable, and realistic to collect from future end users through a short advisory questionnaire.

Modeling

To estimate the risk tolerance of investors, we implemented a Random Forest regression model using the features described in the previous section. Because of the continuous nature of the target variable, the problem was framed as a supervised regression task rather than a categorical classification.

Training was conducted using the combined SCF implicate dataset, with survey weights incorporated during model fitting so that observations contributed in proportion to their representation in the broader U.S. household population. The dataset was partitioned into training and testing subsets to evaluate out-of-sample predictive performance.

The Random Forest output is further transformed via a QuantileTransformer to produce a near-normal risk score distribution on a [1, 10] scale. The QuantileTransformer is fitted on training-set predictions rather than ground-truth labels, ensuring the transformation reflects the model's actual output range and avoids leakage from the test distribution.

2.4.2 Volatility Forecasting

We began our approach to volatility forecasting with data cleaning, imputation of missing or erroneous values, transformation from raw data to useful features, and time-series resampling. We then performed feature selection based on suggestions from the literature and our own correlation analysis. The curious reader can find details of these steps in the appendix. Two features consistently ranked high in feature importance or correlation with future volatility:

  • historical (past 5-day) volatility for the target ETF -- by far the most important feature
  • historical (past 5-day) return for the target ETF

We then resampled all features to a weekly frequency in order to incorporate macroeconomic other lower frequency financial data. To reduce multicollinearity and noise among the expanded feature set, we performed feature extraction using Principal Component Analysis (PCA), capturing 80 percent of the variation in the original set of 87 features in 12 principal features.

We then selected three types of models for comparison which rely on different regression assumptions and methods:

  • a Ridge regressor -- linear regression with L2 norm penalty term
  • a Gradient Boosting regressor (GBR) -- decision tree based ensemble of many weak learners
  • a LSTM deep-learning model -- adept at learning non-linear relationships over time

We paired these three types of models with three different subsets of features, with the idea that the different model types might perform better or worse depending on the subset of input features provided:

  • a "limited" subset consisting of historical (5-day) return and historical (5-day) volatility
  • the "full" feature set, consisting of all 87 features
  • a "PCA" subset, consisting of 12 extracted features

Overall we compared the performance of nine different models. For a more detailed discussion of our methodology and approach to volatility forecasting, refer to the appendix.

Note that the LSTM volatility forecasting component was trained and evaluated on a universe of 21 ETFs, including broad market ETFs. The portfolio optimizer uses a subset of 17 instruments selected to span equities, fixed income, and real assets while using a consistent price history from December 2015 onward.

For portfolio construction, we implemented a constrained mean-variance optimizer grounded in the Markowitz framework mentioned in the Literature review. This choice is deliberate despite well documented sensitivity to input estimates. Markowitz optimization remains the industry standard for systematic portfolio construction with strong interpretability and it provides a principled mechanism for incorporating hard constraints on both volatility and asset weights. In future research, we may consider more sophisticated alternatives like the Black-Litterman model or more robust optimization. That seemed above-and-beyond the scope of this proof of concept project with end-to-end integration. Expected returns on each instrument are estimated via a Sharpe-prior method where the LSTM volatility forecast scales a Sharpe ratio assumption back out to a forward-looking return estimate rather than relying on historical returns alone. The optimizer is implemented using the Python package scipy, with constraints and objective functions parameterized by risk assessment.

Experimental Evaluation

3.1 Evaluation Strategy

Risk Tolerance

Model performance was assessed using regression metrics such as R-squared and root mean squared error (RMSE), allowing us to evaluate both explanatory power and prediction accuracy.

Volatility Forecasting

We conducted hyperparameter tuning for all models (details in the appendix) and compared performance against the validation set for model selection. We used mean absolute error (MAE) as the primary metric to gauge the model's accuracy, but also developed three other criteria (Tracking, Lag, and Estimation -- see appendix for details) to ensure the final model performed according to the business-case requirements of a financial advisor. Based on these criteria, we would select a combination of model type (Ridge, GBR, or LSTM) and feature set ("limited," "full," or "PCA") for a more robust statistical analysis using an ensemble of 30 models for each ETF, details of which are provided in the appendix.

3.2 Results

Risk Tolerance Estimation

Model Selection

We evaluated 2 approaches for predicting risk tolerance. The first used Random Forest regression to predict a latent financial risk tolerance score using PCA. The second used a generalized linear modeling (GLM) framework based on the approach used by Pessoa, Damico, and Jacob (2024) to predict survey respondents’ self-reported risk tolerance score. In the Pessoa et al. approach, we first applied LASSO to select features associated with the self-reported risk score, then fit a weighted linear regression model that accounted for the survey weights and multiple imputations.

We ultimately selected the Random Forest regression model as it achieved a higher R-squared and lower root mean squared error (RMSE), explaining 73% of the variance in the PCA-based risk tolerance score. Although the GLM was not chosen as the final model, we used its most statistically significant predictors to inform the set of variables included in the PCA.

Observations

Figure (2) displays survey-weighted violin plots of the quantile-transformed risk tolerance scores predicted by the Random Forest regression model across each household characteristic. Continuous factors were binned into weighted quantile ranges, while categorical factors were shown as discrete groups, with categories ordered from lower to highest values from left to right within each factor. This facilitates comparison of how the predicted risk tolerance distribution shifts across subgroups. The distributions indicate that married respondents, highest net-worth households, and respondents who report spending less than their income tend to have higher predicted risk tolerance scores, as shown by a rightward shift in the score distribution relative to their comparison groups.

Statistical Measures of Performance

As shown in Figure (3), the LSTM model using a "limited" feature set consistently achieved a lower MAE than the baseline model (VIX) on the testing set for 20 of 21 sector-representative ETFs (all except the 1-3 month treasury bill ETF, ticker: BIL). While LSTM models using the "PCA" feature set achieved a slightly lower median MAE over 30 trials for 11 of 21 ETFs (compared to a LSTM model using "limited" features), in general the "limited" feature set resulted in a tighter distribution, reflecting lower variance in predictions and improved model consistency. By contrast, MAE for a LSTM model using the "full" feature set resulted in a much wider distribution, reflecting higher variance in predictions.

Portfolio Generation

A limitation in our project is that the portfolio optimizer cannot be evaluated against a held-out test set in the conventional sense. Its performance by nature is measurable only through live deployment and longitudinal backtesting on actual performance.What can be assessed at this stage are the structural properties of its outputs. Across ten synthetic client profiles spanning radically different ranges, the optimizer consistently produced feasible, constraint-satisfying portfolios without triggering the volatility fallback. Age remains the dominant allocation driver, with the risk score introducing a calibrated range above and below that driver. Conservative profiles received heavier fixed-income and aggressive profiles permitted greater equity concentration. With this, the optimizer successfully produced risk-adjusted portfolios demonstrating that the constraint architecture is functioning as intended.

§ 04

Discussion

4.1 Interpretation of results

We found that an ensemble LSTM model using a limited feature set (historical return and historical volatility) consistently matched or exceeded the performance of our baseline model (VIX) for 20 of 21 selected ETFs on the held-out testing set, based on our four-fold criteria of Accuracy, Tracking, Lag, and Estimation. Rather than a single model that looks at the market broadly, we created a set of 21 ensemble models (each ensemble consisting of 30 individual LSTM models), where each ensemble model predicts a sector-specific, 30-day future volatility for a representative ETF with accuracy on par with or superior to the baseline model (VIX). The insight that a financial advisor would gain from confident predictions of individual market sectors provides agility in portfolio management: with the insight gained, a financial advisor can confidently move investment dollars between assets depending on their predicted future volatility. This agility is part of the value proposition for our portfolio generation tool.

4.2 Error Analysis

In predicting volatility, during the early model evaluation stage, we found that additional features in the "full" and "PCA" feature sets tended to cause the LSTM models to make more erratic predictions that were out of sync with the true realized volatility trend, reducing the "tracking" score for those models. While we did not have direct insight into which features the LSTM relied on, we found that Ridge and GBR models placed relatively high weight on macroeconomic variables such as yields, change in real GDP, the producer price index change, and change in debt-to-interest ratio. We might infer that the additional features essentially created additional noise, causing the LSTM to overfit to spurious correlations in the training set.

4.3 Limitations

Ultimately, the portfolio optimizer is dictated by the quality of its inputs. Any errors in volatility forecasts or risk scores propagate directly into the resulting portfolio weights with no mechanism for correction downstream. Maximizing the Sharpe ratio is also a non-convex optimization problem, which means that we cannot guarantee a globally optimal solution. Multi-start Dirichlet initialization mitigates this but does not eliminate it. Finally, the pipeline as a whole has not been benchmarked against a real client cohort or compared empirically to an existing robo-advisor. End-to-end evaluation remains the most critical next step before any real-world deployment could be considered.

In the current implementation, if the Random Forest model is unavailable for any reason, the application falls back to a general score based on age, income, and savings behavior that should be surfaced explicitly to any user running the pipeline. The LSTM does not capture macroeconomic dynamics that drive high-yield bond volatility. To compensate, we set a floor at 6% volatility which is quite conservative to the typical realized range of 8-12%. This is a domain-knowledge correction applied post-predication and is a documented and known limitation of our model.

§ 05

Conclusion

5.1 Application and Benefits of the Portfolio Generator Tool

The primary value of our pipeline is delivering what most robo-advisors do not, a transparent and personalized forward looking portfolio construction process. Every assumption is traceable and surfaced through an interactive client dashboard rather than obscured behind a black box. Because the pipeline produces clients with subtly different financial profiles receive meaningfully different portfolios, not the same generic allocations.

The optimizer’s use of near-term volatility forecasts rather than historical averages means portfolio construction responds to current market conditions. Critically, the dashboard communicates uncertainty honestly with Monte Carlo simulations visualized using spaghetti plots and not just a single projected return line that implies false precision. This gives clients a realistic picture of both the upside potential and downside risk of their recommended portfolio, which is the kind of transparency that should be standard in financial advising but rarely is.

5.2 Broader Impacts and Ethical Concerns

This is a proof of concept pipeline and should not be treated as a substitute for licensed financial advice. A client acting on this data may be investing retirement savings or educational funds and could face material harm if the models underperform, inputs are stale, or risk classification is miscalibrated. The nature of the pipelines quantitative precision can also imply a certainty the underlying models do not warrant.

The SCF’s representation also deserves scrutiny. Although the Federal Reserve applies survey weights to approximate the US population, the survey oversamples high-wealth households and relies on voluntary self-reporting. Risk scores derived from this data may systematically underperform for demographics underrepresented in the survey, raising fairness concerns for the very investors this tool aims to serve.

Responsible deployment would require clear disclaimers about model limitations, regular revalidation as market conditions evolve, and a recommended threshold of professional oversight before any client acts on the pipeline’s output. These concerns directly informed our decision to deploy the dashboard as a local application rather than a publicly accessible web service.

§ A

Literature Review

As part of the work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2003, Engle (1982) showed that market volatility is not constant but changes over time. Since then, many studies have demonstrated successful estimation of market volatility using LSTM deep-learning models, which Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997) designed to learn long-range relationships in time-series or sequential data. Several teams have demonstrated success in the past few years using LSTM models of various architectures to forecast market volatility. Assaf, et al. (2022) found that an LSTM model was able to forecast overall market volatility with performance on par with modern econometric models based on the ARCH process. Zhang, et al. (2025) combined a hybrid deep-learning model (consisting of a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) layer, a bidirectional LSTM layer, and an attention mechanism) with an ARCH-based econometric model to forecast volatility of the Shanghai Composite Index (SSEC), claiming to outperform a variety of alternative models including decision tree based ensembles. Most recently, Peter, et al. (2026) integrated a generalized ARCH model with an LSTM model in a stacked ensemble, claiming to demonstrate "consistent superiority" across a variety of metrics.

The foundation of modern portfolio construction is Harry Markowitz’s 1952 mean-variance framework. Markowitz demonstrated that a rational investor, given a set of assets with known expected returns and covariances, should select a portfolio that maximises their return for a given level of variance. Variance can be directly related to risk, so this can viewed equally as minimizing risk for a given expected return of the portfolio. In theory, he suggests that there is an "efficient frontier" of portfolios that minimize risk for all expected returns and any portfolio lying below this line is considered suboptimal because a better risk-return is achievable (Markowitz, 1952).

A key metric derived from this framework is the risk-adjusted return (Sharpe ratio), which normalizes excess return above the risk-free rate (i.e., return on a no-risk asset) by portfolio volatility (Sharpe, 1966). Maximizing the Sharpe ratio is a common objective in portfolio management because it selects the portfolio on the efficient frontier that offers the greatest return per unit of risk.

Unfortunately, the classical mean-variance framework carries well documented limitations. Estimated expected returns and covariances are sensitive to the historical window and sampling period used, and small variations in inputs can produce dramatically different optimal portfolios. Robust optimizations need to explicitly account for uncertainty in the parameter estimates so that they perform acceptably well across a range of scenarios as opposed to optimizing for a single point estimate.

Practical construction also requires incorporating real-world constraints that the unconstrained efficient frontier ignores. These include upper and lower bounds on individual assets (to prevent extreme concentration in a single asset), sector-level exposure limits, and minimum diversification requirements. These constraints are now standard in applied portfolio management but the choice of constraint is not just technical, it encodes the investment philosophy and risk tolerance of the client. This is precisely the link our pipeline is attempting to formalize, by deriving portfolio constraints directly from an estimated risk score rather than a general risk bucket.

§ B

Datasets

The Survey for Consumer Finances that provides granular information of the financial characteristics of households in the United States. The survey is designed to capture financial behavior of households and includes an oversampling of wealthier households to provide a better representation of segments of the population which hold a disproportionate share of financial assets. SCF contains a broad number of features related to household demographics, for example age, education, marital status, employment income, net worth, liquid assets, housing wealth, debt balances, and retirement savings. It also contains behavioral questions related to financial risk taking, saving habits, spending behavior, and household attitudes toward financial decision-making.

One of the most important features of the SCF is the weighted sampling methodology that it uses to oversample wealthier households, however, the weights are provided within each sample so that analyses can be adjusted to reflect the broader U.S. household population rather than the provided raw sample. Another distinguishing feature of the SCF is the use of five imputations to address missing survey responses. Each of the five implicates contains the same households, but missing values are filled in using statistically viable estimates in five different fashions.

From Yahoo Finance we obtained time series data consisting of daily closing prices for 21 ETFs going back as far as January 1993 to the present day. We also collected daily time series data for a variety of indices to provide additional context surrounding daily ETF price changes. These contextual indices include the VIX and other volatility indices, currency indices, commodities such as gold and crude oil, and foreign markets.

From the FRED database, we collected macroeconomic variables such as gross domestic product (GDP), national debt, inflation, unemployment and jobless claims, and treasury yields (ranging from one month to 30 years). These series came in different frequencies -- typically quarterly, monthly, or weekly.

Raw data from Yahoo Finance and the FRED were generally clean, with only a single erroneous value (a negative price), which we corrected by linear interpolation. Several non-ETF indices had missing values, the highest being the Hang Seng Index (^HSI) at 9.7 percent. These missing values were filled using linear interpolation.

Risk Tolerance Estimation

Additional transformations to the SCF data were applied to further prepare the data for modeling: directionality of selected variables was adjusted so that higher values aligned consistently with greater financial risk tolerance, specifically before conducting PCA on some of the variables to construct our target. Missing values were reviewed and handled within the preprocessing workflow, and numeric variables were structured specifically for supervised learning methods. Survey weights were preserved throughout the cleaning process so that they could be incorporated during model training and better reflect the broader population of U.S. households.

Because SCF contains thousands of variables, many of the fields were overly specific or not directly related to investor risk profiling. A more focused feature set was necessary to improve the interpretability and usability of our platform.

For the target variable, PCA was used as a feature extraction method to combine multiple behavioral variables into a latent measure of financial risk tolerance. Variables related to spending more than income, spending less than income, saving tendencies, and perceptions of household financial conditions were selected because they captured behavioral tendencies associated with financial risk tolerance or aversion. Before running PCA, we used LASSO regression to select variables most strongly associated with a self-reported risk tolerance score, ensuring that only relevant predictors informed the composite measure.

Separate feature selection was then conducted for the Random Forest model used to predict the PCA-derived risk score. For this method, emphasis was placed on variables that were meaningful from an economic perspective, explainable, and realistic to collect from future end users through a short advisory questionnaire.

The objective of the Random Forest model was to learn the relationship between observable household characteristics and the latent risk tolerance score derived from the behavioral survey responses.

Random Forest was selected because it is suited for tabular data and can capture non-linear relationships that are common in finance data. Random Forest models are also robust when handling outliers, less sensitive to feature scaling, and capable of handling correlated predictors, which were all factors that contributed to the success of the model.

The architecture consisted of multiple decision trees trained on samples of the training data. At each split, a random subset of predictors was considered, which helps reduce overfitting and improved generalization by diversifying the trees within the ensemble.

The Random Forest output is further transformed via a QuantileTransformer to produce a near-normal risk score distribution on a [1, 10] scale. The QuantileTransformer is fitted on training-set predictions rather than ground-truth labels, ensuring the transformation reflects the model's actual output range and avoids leakage from the test distribution.

C.1 A Brief Introduction to Return and Volatility

Return reflects the change in an asset's price over a period of time. For a given asset, we consider its return at time t to be a random variable of unknown distribution, with zero mean and unknown variance. We used log return, computed as:

Rt = ln( St / St−1 )

where St is the price of the asset at time t.

Volatility reflects the variance in an asset's return over a period of time, and while there are also several methods available for estimating volatility, we rely on realized volatility:

σ = √( (1/n)  Σi Ri² )

In order to evaluate our models against an appropriate benchmark, we chose to forecast volatility over a 21 business day outlook, which aligns with the forecast period for the VIX, and with the business need of a monthly portfolio rebalancing horizon.

C.2 Volatility Forecasting: Detailed Methodology

Data Cleaning, Imputation, and Transformation

Overall we found financial data to be very clean, with at most 9.7 percent missing values for one of the indices. We chose to fill missing values using linear interpolation for indices with daily frequency, while for missing values resulting from upsampling lower-frequency data we used forward fill to guard against data leakage.

The literature widely suggests that recent returns and recent volatility (most notably over the last 5 days) have high correlation with near-term future volatility. Since neither volatility nor future return can be directly observed at the current timestamp, we computed these features from daily log return data.

To make macroeconomic variables such as gross domestic product (GDP) and other contextual data more stationary, we computed either first differences or percentage changes between observations.

We held the final 61 weeks of data in reserve as a testing set, as well as a separate validation set with 85 weeks of data for model tuning and selection. The training set consisted of 371 weekly observations.

Feature Selection and Extraction

With raw data transformed to more useful features, we conducted exploratory data analysis (EDA) to evaluate which features might be most useful in creating an accurate volatility forecast.

In order to reduce the multicollinearity in our data and improve the overall signal-to-noise ratio of our input features, we grouped features that displayed high correlation with each other and then applied PCA.

Modeling

We selected three types of models for analysis (a Ridge regressor, a Gradient Boosting regressor, and a LSTM deep learning model) and compared their performance using three different subsets of features.

A Ridge model applies a linear regression with an L2 norm penalty term to predict the endogenous variable. The Gradient Boosting regressor relaxes the linearity assumption by relying on decision-tree based weak learners. The LSTM assumes neither a linear relationship nor independence and is specifically designed for time-series dependencies.

C.4 Model Selection Criteria: Low vs. High Tracking Score

We developed a four-fold model selection criteria based on the business-case for a financial advisor. Only the first criterion (Accuracy) is completely objective.

Accuracy. The final model should achieve R2OOS above zero (i.e., a smaller MAE than the baseline model).

Tracking. The model's predictions should generally track with realized volatility, showing a similar profile when plotted over time, such that the model appropriately predicts a spike or decline in volatility.

Lag. The model's predictions should not lag excessively behind realized volatility.

Estimation. The model should neither consistently over-estimate nor under-estimate future volatility, although over-estimation is preferred to under-estimation.

C.5 Hyperparameter Tuning

Sampling frequency of time-series variables was a key hyperparameter for all models. After experimenting with several options, we chose to resample all variables to a weekly frequency.

For the LSTM models, hidden layer size, sequence length, and number of training epochs were key hyperparameters. We evaluated performance of stacked LSTMs as well.

For the Ridge regressor the main hyperparameter was alpha in scikit-learn. For the GBR we ran a grid search over learning rate, number of estimators, and tree depth.
