The Risky Business of Financial Matchmaking:

Optimizing Portfolio Return and Volatility Within Investor Risk Tolerance

Authors Peter King, Ryan Peet, Kristine Zhou, Thomas MacPherson Course SIADS 699 - Capstone Status Draft
§ 01

Introduction

1.1 Overview

Financial advising is traditionally expensive, inconsistent, and largely inaccessible to investors without significant wealth. While robo-advisors have begun to automate the process, most continue to operate as black boxes. Meaning, they rely on coarse risk categories and obscuring assumptions behind their recommendations. This project introduces a three-component pipeline to address this gap. First, a risk tolerance classifier that produces a continuous client risk score from nationally representative survey data. Secondly, a volatility forecasting model whose predictions serve as inputs to a Sharpe-prior return estimation method, together producing forward-looking expected returns for each instrument. Finally a constrained mean-variance optimizer that combines these inputs into an individualized, risk-calibrated portfolio. The central contribution is not any single model but the end-to-end integration of all three, with results presented through an interactive client dashboard.

1.2.2 Volatility Forecasting

Prior to Engle (1982) and his introduction of the autoregressive conditional heteroscedastic (ARCH) class of stochastic processes, econometric models assumed a "constant one-period forecast variance." Engle's observation that the future variance of a stochastic process depends on recent past values paved the way for new approaches in volatility forecasting. Many recent studies have successfully employed Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) deep-learning models to estimate future volatility, exploiting such models' ability to learn dependencies and relationships between variables over time (Assaf, et al. (2022), Zhang, et al. (2025), and Peter, et al. (2026)).

Our study takes a novel approach by focusing on volatility for specific market sectors in the U.S. economy, represented by a selection of exchange traded funds (ETFs), rather than overall market volatility. Furthermore, we establish a practical benchmark by comparing our model's performance to the Cboe Volatility Index (ticker: ^VIX) -- widely considered the "the most popular gauge" of future (30-day) market volatility (Schwab, 2025).

1.2.3 Portfolio Optimization

Portfolio construction in this project is grounded in Markowitz’s (1952) mean variance framework, which states that a rational investor should try to maximize their return for a given level of risk. A common metric derived from this is the Sharpe ratio, which measures return per unit of risk above a risk-free baseline (Sharpe, 1966). In practice, classic mean-variance optimization is highly sensitive to input estimates, often producing unstable portfolios (Michaud, 1989), and requires boundaries on individual assets and specific diversification requirements to prevent extreme concentration in a single asset. In this pipeline, those constraints are driven directly from a modelled client risk score.

§ 02

Problem Definition, Approach, and Methodology

2.1 The Challenge

The challenge was to build an integrated data science pipeline that translates individualized investor profiles into a personalized investment strategy through risk scoring, volatility forecasting, and constrained portfolio optimization.

2.2 Sub-Problems

2.2.1 Estimating Client Risk Tolerance

For the first component of our portfolio generation pipeline, our goal was to predict an individual investor’s risk tolerance using household and demographic characteristics which were observed within the Survey for Consumer Finances (SCF). Understanding a client’s appetite for risk is important in the application of any portfolio recommendation system because it influences how a portfolio should be balanced between pursuing larger returns and limiting potential losses. Because the SCF contains many non-correlated variables which could affect how an individual perceives financial risk, but no direct measure of financial risk-taking, our goal with the risk tolerance estimation and model was to use surveyable household features to estimate how much investment risk an individual may be comfortable taking.

2.2.2 Forecasting Sector Volatility

For the second component of our portfolio generation pipeline, our goal was to forecast realized volatility (also referred to as historical volatility) for a collection of sector-representative ETFs over a 21 business day (30 calendar day) outlook. A brief discussion of return and volatility is included in the appendix to this report. Our goals were to achieve forecast accuracy on par or superior to the VIX for the S&P 500 ETF (ticker: SPY) and to create sector-specific forecasts with comparable accuracy to what the VIX achieves for the S&P 500.

2.2.3 Maximising Return while Limiting Portfolio Volatility

Given a client's estimated risk score and a set of sector-level volatility forecasts, the third component constructs a portfolio designed to maximise risk-adjusted return within constraints calibrated to that individual. The risk score regulates the full constraint architecture through a two-tier system.

Tier 1 — Asset-Class Level
  • Age-adjusted equity ceiling (110 − age baseline, modified by risk score)
  • Gold cap inverse-scaled with risk score (15% at score 1 → 5% at score 10)
  • Volatility band: floor and ceiling both scale linearly with risk score
Tier 2 — Intra-Bucket Level
  • Any single equity sector ≤ 30% of total equity bucket
  • Any IG fixed-income instrument ≤ 50% of fixed-income allocation
  • HYG ≤ (risk score / 10)² × 35% of FI bucket (quadratic scaling)

The quadratic HYG cap reflects the fiduciary view that high-yield credit exposure should be strictly proportional to demonstrated risk tolerance. More conservative profiles receive tighter volatility ceilings and greater diversification requirements; more aggressive profiles are permitted higher concentrations and volatility in exchange for greater return potential.

The optimiser is designed to maximise the Sharpe ratio of the resulting portfolio. For a portfolio with weight vector w, expected return vector μ, and covariance matrix Σ, the objective is:

maximise    (wTμ − rf)  /  √(wTΣw)

This is subject to constraints on total portfolio volatility, individual weight bounds, sector exposure limits, and a full investment constraint (weights sum to one; no short positions). The output is a set of portfolio weights per ETF representing the recommended allocation — not the universally optimal portfolio, but the portfolio that theoretically maximises risk-adjusted return given the specific client's risk profile and current volatility forecasts.

A client reclassified from moderate to more conservative — whether due to different survey responses or a model misclassification — will receive a meaningfully different portfolio with a lower volatility ceiling and greater diversification requirements under the same volatility environment. The sensitivity of the portfolio output to risk classification is substantial, as this is what drives individual portfolio construction.

2.3 Datasets

2.3.1 Survey of Consumer Finances (2022)

The Survey for Consumer Finances (SCF) is a survey conducted every three years by the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System that provides granular information of the financial characteristics of households in the United States. The survey is designed to capture financial behavior of households and includes an oversampling of wealthier households to provide a better representation of segments of the population which hold a disproportionate share of financial assets. SCF contains a broad number of features related to household demographics, for example age, education, marital status, employment income, net worth, liquid assets, housing wealth, debt balances, and retirement savings. It also contains behavioral questions related to financial risk taking, saving habits, spending behavior, and household attitudes toward financial decision-making.

One of the most important features of the SCF is the weighted sampling methodology that it uses to oversample wealthier households, however, the weights are provided within each sample so that analyses can be adjusted to reflect the broader U.S. household population rather than the provided raw sample. Another distinguishing feature of the SCF is the use of five imputations to address missing survey responses. Each of the five implicates contains the same households, but missing values are filled in using statistically viable estimates in five different fashions. This better reflects the uncertainty by allowing researchers to analyze all five imputations separately or all at once, which can lead to more reliable results depending on the analysis method used in the research.

2.3.2 Financial Data: Yahoo Finance & FRED

Our financial data came from two sources: Yahoo Finance (via the yfinance Python library) and the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) database (via the fredapi Python library). For a detailed description of these datasets, including date ranges, frequency, and quality, refer to Appendix B.

2.4 Methodology

2.4.1 Risk Tolerance Estimation

Data cleaning, imputation, and transformation

Our final Python methodology encompassed the download of the SCF Stata data directly from the Federal Reserve website, which included the primary survey data, supplemental extract files, and replicate weight files used for survey analysis. Each dataset was then standardized to consistent lowercase column names and then validated to ensure row counts and identifiers were consistent across files.

One of the main preprocessing steps involved separating the SCF into each of its five implicates, where each implicate was extracted using the household identifier structure and then merged with the supplementary data using common keys. Once the data was verified, the five implicates were concatenated into a single modeling dataset to preserve the full range of imputed household responses in the survey.

Additional transformations were then applied to further prepare the data for modeling: directionality of selected variables was adjusted so that higher values aligned consistently with greater financial risk tolerance, specifically before conducting PCA on some of the variables to construct our target. Missing values were reviewed and handled within the preprocessing workflow, and numeric variables were structured specifically for supervised learning methods. Survey weights were preserved throughout the cleaning process so that they could be incorporated during model training and better reflect the broader population of U.S. households.

Feature selection and extraction

Because SCF contains thousands of variables, many of the fields were overly specific or not directly related to investor risk profiling. A more focused feature set was necessary to improve the interpretability and usability of our platform.

For the target variable, PCA was used as a feature extraction method to combine multiple behavioral variables into a latent measure of financial risk tolerance. Variables related to spending more than income, spending less than income, saving tendencies, and perceptions of household financial conditions were selected because they captured behavioral tendencies associated with financial risk tolerance or aversion. The feature selection combined with PCA provided a sound proxy target for supervised learning in the absence of a specific measure related to financial risk tolerance.

Separate feature selection was then conducted for the Random Forest model used to predict the PCA-derived risk score. For this method, emphasis was placed on variables that were meaningful from an economic perspective, explainable, and realistic to collect from future end users through a short advisory questionnaire. Inputs included age, education, marital status, number of children, income, net worth, total assets, debt, liquid assets, housing wealth, vehicle ownership, and savings-related measures. These variables were chosen because they reflect life stage and financial capacity, which are likely related to investment risk preferences, and are not likely highly technical or obscure to the end user.

Modeling

To estimate the risk tolerance of investors, we implemented a Random Forest regression model using the features described in the previous section. The objective of this model was to learn the relationship between observable household characteristics and the latent risk tolerance score derived from the behavioral survey responses. Because of the continuous nature of the target variable, the problem was framed as a supervised regression task rather than a categorical classification.

Random Forest was selected because it is suited for tabular data and can capture non-linear relationships that are common in finance data. Random Forest models are also robust when handling outliers, less sensitive to feature scaling, and capable of handling correlated predictors, which were all factors that contributed to the success of the model.

The architecture consisted of multiple decision trees trained on samples of the training data. At each split, a random subset of predictors was considered, which helps reduce overfitting and improved generalization by diversifying the trees within the ensemble. Final predictions were generated by averaging the output across all trees in the forest.

Training was conducted using the combined SCF implicate dataset, with survey weights incorporated during model fitting so that observations contributed in proportion to their representation in the broader U.S. household population. The dataset was partitioned into training and testing subsets to evaluate out-of-sample predictive performance. Model performance was assessed using regression metrics such as R-squared and root mean squared error (RMSE), allowing us to evaluate both explanatory power and prediction accuracy.

The Random Forest output is further transformed via a QuantileTransformer to produce a near-normal risk score distribution on a [1, 10] scale. The QuantileTransformer is fitted on training-set predictions rather than ground-truth labels, ensuring the transformation reflects the model's actual output range and avoids leakage from the test distribution.

2.4.2 Volatility Forecasting

We began our approach to volatility forecasting with data cleaning, imputation of missing or erroneous values, transformation from raw data to useful features, and time-series resampling. Overall, financial data was very clean, with at most 9.7 percent missing values for one index. We filled missing values using linear interpolation for daily-frequency indices; for missing values resulting from upsampling lower-frequency data, we used forward-fill to guard against data leakage.

Feature selection was informed by the literature and our own correlation analysis (details in Appendix C). The two features that consistently ranked highest in importance and correlation with the target variable were:

  • Historical (5-day) volatility for the target ETF — by far the most important predictor
  • Historical (5-day) return for the target ETF

To incorporate lower-frequency macroeconomic data, all time-series features were resampled to weekly frequency. Feature extraction via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was then applied, retaining 80 percent of the variation in the original 87 features in just 12 principal components. Full details are in Appendix C.

We evaluated three model types against three feature subsets — nine combinations in total:

  • Ridge regressor — linear regression with L2 norm penalty
  • Gradient Boosting regressor (GBR) — decision tree ensemble of weak learners
  • LSTM deep-learning model — capable of learning non-linear temporal relationships

Feature subsets were: a limited set (5-day return and 5-day volatility only), the full set (all 87 features), and a PCA set (return, volatility, and 12 PCA components). For a detailed discussion of methodology, refer to Appendix C.

2.4.3 Portfolio Optimisation

Note that the LSTM volatility forecasting component was trained and evaluated on a universe of 21 ETFs, including broad market ETFs. The portfolio optimiser uses a subset of 17 instruments, selected to span equities, fixed income, and real assets while maintaining a consistent price history from December 2015 onward — constrained by the launch date of XLRE (Real Estate).

For portfolio construction, we implemented a constrained mean-variance optimiser grounded in the Markowitz framework. This choice is deliberate despite the well-documented sensitivity to input estimates: Markowitz optimisation remains the industry standard for systematic portfolio construction, offers strong interpretability, and provides a principled mechanism for incorporating hard constraints on both volatility and asset weights. In future research, more sophisticated alternatives like the Black-Litterman model or robust optimisation variants may be considered; these were judged above-and-beyond the scope of this proof-of-concept pipeline. The optimiser is implemented using scipy.optimize.minimize (SLSQP method), with constraints and objective functions parameterised by the client's risk assessment.

Volatility Forecasting

We conducted hyperparameter tuning for all models (details in Appendix C) and compared performance against the validation set for model selection. Mean absolute error (MAE) served as the primary accuracy metric. We also developed three additional criteria to ensure the final model met the business requirements of a financial advisor:

  • Accuracy — R²OOS above zero (smaller MAE than baseline)
  • Tracking — predictions should follow the general volatility profile over time, appropriately anticipating spikes and declines
  • Lag — predictions should not trail realised volatility excessively
  • Estimation — the model should not systematically over- or under-estimate; over-estimation preferred to under-estimation

We compared each model to the VIX as a baseline for predicting the SPY ETF. Out-of-sample R-squared was computed as:

R²OOS = 1 − MAEm / MAEb

where MAEm is model MAE and MAEb is baseline (VIX) MAE. Based on these criteria, we selected a model type and feature set for robust statistical testing: training 30 independent learners per ETF over 30 independent trials, comparing the median MAE distribution against the baseline. This ensemble approach guards against lucky initialisation from stochastic training and produces more reliable predictions than any single model.

§ 04

Discussion

4.1 Interpretation of Results

We found that an ensemble LSTM model using a limited feature set (historical 5-day return and historical 5-day volatility) consistently matched or exceeded the performance of the VIX baseline for 20 of 21 selected ETFs on the held-out testing set, across all four evaluation criteria. Rather than a single model looking at the market broadly, we produced a set of 21 ensemble models — each consisting of 30 independent LSTM learners — where each ensemble predicts a sector-specific, 30-day future volatility with accuracy on par with or superior to the VIX.

The insight that a financial advisor would gain from confident sector-specific volatility predictions provides genuine portfolio management agility: with reliable near-term forecasts, allocation dollars can be systematically moved between assets based on predicted forward volatility rather than historical averages. This sector-level granularity — as opposed to broad market volatility — is part of the core value proposition of the pipeline.

4.2 Error Analysis

During early model evaluation, we found that additional features in the "full" and "PCA" feature sets tended to cause LSTM models to make more erratic predictions that were out of sync with true realised volatility trends, reducing the Tracking score. While we did not have direct insight into which features the LSTM relied on, Ridge and GBR models placed relatively high weight on macroeconomic variables such as yields, change in real GDP, producer price index change, and change in debt-to-interest ratio. We infer that these additional features introduced noise, causing the LSTM to overfit spurious correlations in the training set.

This finding — that a parsimonious two-feature model outperforms a richly-featured one — is consistent with the literature on financial time-series: recent volatility is so dominant a predictor that additional features compete with it as a signal rather than complementing it.

4.3 Limitations

The portfolio optimiser is dictated by the quality of its inputs. Any errors in volatility forecasts or risk scores propagate directly into the resulting portfolio weights with no correction mechanism downstream. Maximising the Sharpe ratio is also a non-convex optimisation problem, meaning a globally optimal solution cannot be guaranteed. Multi-start Dirichlet initialisation mitigates but does not eliminate this.

In the current implementation, if the trained Random Forest model artifact is unavailable at inference time, the application silently falls back to a heuristic score based on age, income, and savings behaviour. This condition should be surfaced explicitly to any user or evaluator running the pipeline for the first time on a new machine.

The LSTM's limited feature set does not capture the credit spread dynamics that materially drive high-yield bond volatility. To compensate, HYG annualised volatility is floored at 6%, which is conservative relative to HYG's typical realised range of 8–12%. This is a domain-knowledge correction applied post-prediction and is a documented, known limitation.

Finally, the pipeline as a whole has not been benchmarked against a real client cohort or compared empirically to an existing robo-advisor. XLRE's December 2015 launch date sets the common correlation history start, meaning the 2008 Global Financial Crisis is absent from the covariance estimates — a stress period with substantially elevated correlations that the model has never seen. End-to-end evaluation against real-world conditions remains the most critical next step before any deployment could be considered.

§ 05

Conclusion

5.1 Application and Benefits of the Portfolio Generator Tool

The primary value of this pipeline is delivering what most robo-advisors do not: a transparent and personalised, forward-looking portfolio construction process. Every assumption is traceable and surfaced through an interactive client dashboard rather than obscured behind a black box. Clients with subtly different financial profiles receive meaningfully different portfolios — not the same generic allocations.

The optimiser's use of near-term volatility forecasts rather than historical averages means portfolio construction responds to current market conditions. Critically, the dashboard communicates uncertainty through Monte Carlo simulation visualised as spaghetti plots, not a single projected return line that implies false precision. This gives clients a realistic picture of both upside potential and downside risk — the kind of transparency that should be standard in financial advising but rarely is.

5.2 Broader Impacts and Ethical Concerns

This is a proof-of-concept pipeline and should not be treated as a substitute for licensed financial advice. A client acting on this output may be investing retirement savings or educational funds, and could face material harm if models underperform, inputs are stale, or risk classification is miscalibrated. The quantitative precision of the pipeline can imply a certainty the underlying models do not warrant.

The SCF's representation deserves scrutiny. Although the Federal Reserve applies survey weights to approximate the U.S. population, the survey oversamples high-wealth households and relies on voluntary self-reporting. Risk scores derived from this data may systematically underperform for demographics underrepresented in the survey, raising fairness concerns for the very investors this tool aims to serve.

Responsible deployment requires clear disclaimers about model limitations, regular revalidation as market conditions evolve, and a threshold of professional oversight before any client acts on the pipeline's output. These concerns directly informed our decision to deploy the dashboard as a local application rather than a publicly accessible web service.

§ A

Literature Review

Several teams have demonstrated success in recent years using LSTM models of various architectures to forecast market volatility. Assaf et al. (2022) found that an LSTM model was able to forecast overall market volatility with performance on par with modern econometric models based on the ARCH process. Zhang et al. (2025) combined a hybrid deep-learning model — consisting of a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) layer, a bidirectional LSTM layer, and an attention mechanism — with an ARCH-based econometric model to forecast volatility of the Shanghai Composite Index (SSEC), claiming to outperform a variety of alternative models including decision tree-based ensembles. Most recently, Peter et al. (2026) integrated a generalised ARCH model with an LSTM model in a stacked ensemble, claiming to demonstrate "consistent superiority" across a variety of metrics.

The foundation of modern portfolio construction is Harry Markowitz’s 1952 mean-variance framework. Markowitz demonstrated that a rational investor, given a set of assets with known expected returns and covariances, should select a portfolio that maximises their return for a given level of variance. Variance can be directly related to risk, so this can viewed equally as minimizing risk for a given expected return of the portfolio. In theory, he suggests that there is an "efficient frontier" of portfolios that minimize risk for all expected returns and any portfolio lying below this line is considered suboptimal because a better risk-return is achievable (Markowitz, 1952).

A key metric derived from this framework is the risk-adjusted return (Sharpe ratio), which normalizes excess return above the risk-free rate (i.e., return on a no-risk asset) by portfolio volatility (Sharpe, 1966). Maximizing the Sharpe ratio is a common objective in portfolio management because it selects the portfolio on the efficient frontier that offers the greatest return per unit of risk.

Unfortunately, the classical mean-variance framework carries well documented limitations. Estimated expected returns and covariances are sensitive to the historical window and sampling period used, and small variations in inputs can produce dramatically different optimal portfolios. Robust optimizations need to explicitly account for uncertainty in the parameter estimates so that they perform acceptably well across a range of scenarios as opposed to optimizing for a single point estimate.

Practical construction also requires incorporating real-world constraints that the unconstrained efficient frontier ignores. These include upper and lower bounds on individual assets (to prevent extreme concentration in a single asset), sector-level exposure limits, and minimum diversification requirements. These constraints are now standard in applied portfolio management but the choice of constraint is not just technical, it encodes the investment philosophy and risk tolerance of the client. This is precisely the link our pipeline is attempting to formalize, by deriving portfolio constraints directly from an estimated risk score rather than a general risk bucket.

§ B

Datasets

From Yahoo Finance, we obtained time-series data consisting of daily closing prices for 21 ETFs, going back as far as January 1993. We also collected daily time-series data for a variety of indices to provide additional context surrounding daily ETF price changes. These contextual indices include the VIX and other volatility indices, currency indices, commodities such as gold and crude oil, and foreign markets.

From the FRED database, we collected macroeconomic variables such as gross domestic product (GDP), national debt, inflation, unemployment and jobless claims, and treasury yields. These series came in different frequencies — typically quarterly, monthly, or weekly.

Raw data from both sources was generally very clean. A single erroneous value (a negative price) was corrected by linear interpolation. Several non-ETF indices had missing values, the highest being the Hang Seng Index (^HSI) at 9.7 percent. These were filled via linear interpolation, with a buffer of 21 business days enforced between training, validation, and test splits to guard against data leakage.

§ C

Volatility Modeling

C.1 A Brief Introduction to Return and Volatility

Return reflects the change in an asset's price over a period of time. For a given asset, we consider its return at time t to be a random variable Rt of unknown distribution with zero mean (μ = 0) and unknown variance (σ²). We used log return, computed as:

Rt = ln( St / St−1 )

where St is the closing price at time t. Volatility reflects the variance in an asset's return over a period of time. We rely on realised volatility — simply the standard deviation of return over a period — which, under the zero-mean assumption, simplifies to:

σ = √( (1/n)  Σi Ri² )

We forecast volatility over a 21 business day (≈ 30 calendar day) outlook, aligning our predictions with the VIX forecast horizon and the business need of monthly portfolio rebalancing.

C.2 Volatility Forecasting: Detailed Methodology

Data Cleaning, Imputation, and Transformation

For each timestamp, we created a near-term realised volatility feature (computed over the last 5 business days) and a future volatility label (computed over the next 21 business days) from daily log return data. Macroeconomic variables were made more stationary by computing first differences or percentage changes. All variables were resampled to weekly frequency — upsampled values were forward-filled rather than interpolated, to prevent the model from accessing future trends during training.

We held the final 61 weeks as a test set, 85 weeks as a validation set, and used 371 weekly observations for training — with 21 business-day buffers between each split to guard against label leakage. Features were standardised to (0, 1) with the scaler fitted only on training data.

Feature Selection and Extraction

EDA confirmed that historical 5-day volatility was highly correlated with future volatility (correlation coefficient = 0.72 for SPY), and the 5-day return showed moderate negative correlation (−0.37 for SPY). VIX and related volatility indices showed similar correlation to future SPY volatility (VIX: 0.75). By contrast, no feature showed strong correlation with future return — the highest being jobless claims at 0.14 — confirming that return forecasting from these features is not viable.

To reduce multicollinearity, features with high inter-correlation were grouped and subjected to PCA, retaining 12 principal components that collectively explain 80% of variance in the original 87 features.

Modeling

We compared nine model combinations (three model types × three feature sets). The Ridge regressor applies L2-regularised linear regression, assuming a linear relationship and independence of observations over time. The Gradient Boosting regressor (GBR) relaxes the linearity assumption but still assumes observation independence. The LSTM assumes neither a linear relationship nor independence — it is specifically designed to learn both long and short-term temporal dependencies.

We speculate that the additional layers in stacked LSTMs, often recommended in the literature for language tasks, are less beneficial for financial volatility regression, where the dominant dependency is simply autocorrelation in recent volatility — a simpler temporal structure than the long-range semantic dependencies that motivate stacking in NLP.

C.4 Model Selection Criteria: Low vs. High Tracking Score

The Tracking criterion captures whether a model's predictions follow the general contour of realised volatility over time — appropriately rising when volatility spikes and declining when it recedes. A model that guesses an average value or produces erratic predictions fails this criterion even if its mean error is acceptable.

C.5 Hyperparameter Tuning

Resampling frequency to weekly was a key decision for all models, striking a balance between the granularity of daily closing prices and the lower-frequency macroeconomic inputs. For LSTM models, key hyperparameters were hidden layer size, sequence length, and number of training epochs. Stacked LSTMs (additional LSTM layers) were evaluated but caused more erratic predictions without consistent improvement, consistent with our hypothesis that financial volatility's temporal dependencies are simpler than those in language tasks.

For Ridge, the L2 regularisation coefficient ("alpha" in scikit-learn) was the primary tuned parameter. For GBR, a grid search was conducted over learning rate, number of estimators, max depth, and max features. Full hyperparameter configurations for all models are documented in the Jupyter notebooks accompanying this report.

Known Limitation — 2008 Crisis Absent from Covariance Estimates The binding constraint on correlation history is the December 2015 launch of XLRE (Real Estate ETF). The 2008 Global Financial Crisis — a period of dramatically elevated cross-asset correlations — falls outside the estimation window. The resulting correlation matrix may underestimate correlation spikes during stress regimes, producing a portfolio that appears more diversified than it would prove to be in a true market crisis.
